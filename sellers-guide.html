Selling your home in New York City

A clear plan for pricing, preparing, marketing and closing—with the details that matter for NYC condos, co-ops and townhouses.

By Glenn Velasco
Licensed Real Estate Salesperson · Compass

Published November 4, 2026

Selling well takes a plan you can understand and decisions you can make with confidence. This guide follows six stages, with the more detailed costs and questions tucked below. Glenn helps you connect the strategy to your home, your schedule and your next move.

1 / Plan

Start with the move you want to make.

Your sale starts with a conversation about timing, priorities and what comes next. Are you buying another home, relocating, simplifying your holdings or coordinating a sale from abroad? We use those answers to build a pricing, preparation and marketing plan around your situation.

Glenn coordinates the market strategy, presentation, showings and negotiations. Bring a New York real-estate attorney into the process early to review the listing agreement, property documents and contract. If you need an introduction, Glenn has a network of attorneys he can connect you with.

The listing agreement should make the services, term, marketing permissions and compensation clear. Agent compensation is negotiable and is not set by law. Review any proposed buyer-agent compensation or seller concessions as part of the overall plan.

A short checklist for our first conversation
  • Your preferred timing and whether the sale connects to another purchase.
  • The home’s condition, renovations and features you want buyers to understand.
  • Current monthly charges, taxes, assessments and any mortgage balance.
  • Building procedures, occupancy and any existing leases.
  • Your preferences for photography, showing access and public exposure.

2 / Price

Price the home buyers will compare.

A useful valuation looks at recent comparable sales, current competition and the details that distinguish your property: layout, condition, floor, exposure, outdoor space, building services and monthly expenses. Asking prices show the alternatives buyers see; completed sales show what other buyers actually paid.

We review a supported price range and choose a launch price with a clear rationale. A neighborhood average or an automated estimate can start the conversation, but it cannot fully account for a particular apartment or building. Start with a free home valuation, then refine it through a property review.

Your neighborhood is part of what you are selling. The walk to transit, nearby outdoor space and everyday conveniences help buyers understand how the home fits their lives. We describe those features accurately and connect them to the property, with neighborhood guides available for buyers who want more context.

What happens if the response is different from expectations?

We look at inquiries, completed showings, repeat visits and the substance of buyer feedback alongside changes in competing inventory. Then we decide whether presentation, access, marketing or price needs adjustment. Views alone do not establish demand, and an early reaction from a small audience is not a full market test.

3 / Prepare

Make the home—and the paperwork—ready.

Begin with the improvements that help buyers understand the space: cleaning, decluttering, lighting, touch-ups and targeted repairs. We consider staging and photography around the home’s layout and likely presentation needs. A major renovation is a separate decision; compare its budget, timing and uncertainty before committing.

Preparation also means getting answers ready. Buyers and their attorneys will ask about ownership, charges, building condition and proposed work. Having the relevant records organized can make those conversations more useful.

Condominium

Gather current common charges, taxes, assessments and renovation records. Coordinate with management on available building financials, governing documents, application requirements and transfer procedures. Your attorney will confirm what the buyer needs and any right-of-first-refusal waiver process.

Co-op

Locate the stock and proprietary-lease information, current maintenance and assessments, and the building’s sales application and fee schedule. Confirm board procedures, any transfer fee or “flip tax,” and move-out requirements with management. Missing originals or unclear fees are better addressed before a closing is scheduled.

Townhouse or multifamily home

Organize the deed, occupancy records, renovation permits, system information and any leases, deposits and operating expenses. Ask your attorney which disclosures apply. New York’s property-condition disclosure requirements generally cover qualifying one-to-four-family homes, with exceptions; condominium units and co-op apartments are excluded from that statute’s definition. Other disclosure duties may still apply. Use the current state form with your attorney.

Could Compass Concierge help with preparation?

Compass Concierge may help eligible sellers finance approved preparation services such as painting, staging and repairs. Discuss the scope and current written terms before choosing it.

Approval is not guaranteed, fees or interest may apply, and repayment can become due before a sale—for example, if the listing ends or the agreement’s time limit is reached. It is financing, not a grant, and it does not guarantee a particular sale result.

4 / Market

Choose how your home enters the market.

The launch plan brings together the photography, property description, floor plan, showing arrangements and agreed marketing materials. Glenn can coordinate property-specific outreach and digital and print presentation, with the channels and scope agreed before launch.

We also discuss the balance between broad exposure and privacy. You choose the approach after reviewing the options, their tradeoffs and the current local listing-service and distribution requirements.

Private marketing and a public launch

A Compass Private Exclusive may be an option for a seller who wants a limited initial audience or more control over public exposure. It can provide early conversations and feedback, but it reaches fewer buyers than a broad public launch and can limit competition.

A public launch aims for broader visibility through the applicable listing service and participating websites. Availability, timing and distribution depend on the plan and current rules. A private phase is a choice to discuss, not a required first step or a promise of a higher price.

How we keep track of the response

We agree on a communication rhythm and review showing activity, questions, feedback and competing listings. Compass One can bring the timeline, documents, communication and available listing-engagement information into one place. Those signals inform our discussion; they do not replace judgment about actual offers.

5 / Negotiate

Compare the terms behind the number.

The strongest offer for your situation depends on more than price. Review financing, supporting financial information, contingencies, the proposed schedule, included property and any requested concessions. For an apartment sale, consider the building’s application requirements and the buyer’s readiness to complete the process.

Glenn helps you compare and negotiate the commercial terms; your attorney prepares and negotiates the legal contract. An accepted offer is generally not yet a binding NYC sale. Your attorney confirms when the contract has been properly signed and delivered and what obligations apply.

Contract out means the contract has been sent. Contract signed means it has been signed. Keep those milestones separate when planning your next move.

Preparing for buyer due diligence

Coordinate access for any agreed inspection and respond through the appropriate professionals to questions about the property and building. Your attorney handles legal disclosures, contract protections and any negotiated repair or credit. The exact order depends on the property and agreement.

6 / Close

Keep the next steps moving together.

After the contract is signed, track the remaining requirements: the buyer’s financing and appraisal where applicable, building approvals or waivers, title or lien issues, payoff arrangements and the closing calendar. Glenn stays involved with the transaction team while your attorney handles the legal closing work.

Before the final walk-through, complete the obligations in the contract and confirm the agreed condition and possession arrangements. Coordinate the move, building reservations, keys and access devices. Your attorney confirms the final figures and closing instructions.

There is no single reliable timeline for every NYC sale. Preparation, buyer financing, building procedures and legal issues can each change the schedule. Plan around confirmed milestones, especially when a sale funds your next purchase.

Your budget

Focus on what you will keep.

The sale price is the starting point. To estimate your proceeds, subtract agreed selling expenses, transfer taxes, mortgage and lien payoffs, and closing adjustments. A mortgage payoff reduces the cash you receive, but it is repayment of debt rather than a service fee.

At a glance: for a typical taxable NYC condo, co-op or one-to-three-family home sale, combined city and state seller transfer taxes are approximately 1.4% at $500,000 or less; 1.825% above $500,000 and below $3 million; and 2.075% at $3 million or more. These are transfer taxes only—not your total selling costs. Exemptions, state rounding rules and transaction terms can change the calculation.

See transfer-tax details

NYC residential transfer tax is 1% at $500,000 or less and 1.425% above $500,000 for qualifying transfers. The residential category covers individual condos, co-op apartments and one-to-three-family houses. Tax generally applies when consideration exceeds $25,000.

New York State’s base transfer tax is $2 per $500 of consideration or fraction thereof. For qualifying NYC residential transfers at $3 million or more, an additional $1.25 per $500 applies. These correspond to approximately 0.4% and 0.65%. Mansion and supplemental taxes are generally the buyer’s responsibility; contract terms and statutory exceptions matter.

For illustration, a qualifying $1.5 million resale produces $21,375 in city transfer tax and $6,000 in state base tax: $27,375 total, before other selling expenses. Four-family, larger multifamily, mixed-use and other property categories can follow different rates; ask your attorney for the property-specific calculation.

Other expenses to include
  • Agreed agent compensation: use the written agreement, including any authorized buyer-agent payment. There is no legally fixed commission.
  • Attorney and closing charges: obtain a quote and allow for payoff, filing or other transaction-specific charges.
  • Building fees: confirm managing-agent charges, move-out fees or deposits, and any co-op or condo transfer fee.
  • Preparation and moving: include approved repairs, staging, storage and relocation costs.
  • Adjustments and concessions: allow for prorations and any credits agreed in the contract.

Capital-gains tax and any withholding require a separate discussion with your tax adviser. A closing net sheet is not a complete income-tax calculation.

International sellers

Selling while you are outside New York.

Set up the practical details early: access to the home, time zones, document signing, building coordination and how closing funds will be received. Glenn works in English, Italian, Spanish and French and can coordinate the property side while your attorney confirms the legal signing arrangements.

If the seller is a foreign person for U.S. tax purposes, FIRPTA withholding may affect the funds released at closing. Tax status is not determined by language or nationality alone. Have your attorney and tax adviser review the applicable withholding, exceptions and filing steps before you rely on a net-proceeds estimate.

Frequently asked questions

A few questions before you begin.

Do I need to renovate before selling?

Not always. Start with the improvements that clarify the home’s condition and layout. Compare the cost and timing of larger work against the likely benefit before committing.

Do I have to start with a Private Exclusive?

No. We discuss private and public marketing options, the tradeoffs and current local requirements. The launch plan should reflect your priorities and informed choice.

What is a co-op flip tax?

It is a building-specific transfer fee, not a city or state tax. Its amount, calculation and responsibility depend on the governing documents and transaction. Confirm it with management and your attorney.

Can I sell and buy another home at the same time?

Yes, but the timing and funding need a coordinated plan. Discuss whether your next purchase depends on sale proceeds, how you will handle a gap between closings and what contractual protections are appropriate.

Is an accepted offer the same as a signed contract?

No. In a typical NYC transaction, the attorneys still need to negotiate and complete the contract. Your attorney confirms when it becomes binding.

Does Compass Concierge mean I only repay when the home sells?

Not necessarily. Other repayment triggers can apply, including termination of the listing or a time limit. Review the current written agreement, eligibility requirements and any fees or interest before using the program.
